What Are Trigger and Content Warnings?
Purpose: To clearly define what a Trigger and Content Warning is.

Definition
- Trigger and Content Warnings give readers of your work, pictures, videos, replies, or
posts an overview of what to expect. Think of them as a heads-up before you dive into
reading or viewing something.
- Everyone has something that can cause them negative feelings, even more so if you
already had a rough day or week. Trigger and Content Warnings are supposed to help you
make an educated decision if you want to engage with the content, either in the moment
or in general.
- They make content easier to navigate and more accessible for people with certain
sensitivities or mental health problems such as PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder).

What Is a Trigger Warning (TW)?
- Trigger Warnings (TWs) are used to warn the reader or viewer about potentially
triggering1 content. (See here for a list of common Trigger Warnings.)
- Rule of Thumb: If the content is (usually) traumatic for a human or animal, it requires a
Trigger Warning.

What Is a Content Warning (CW)?
- Content Warnings (CWs) are used to warn the reader or viewer about potentially
upsetting or sensitive content. (See here for a list of common Content Warnings.)
- Rule of Thumb: If the content needs to be treated with care or upsets many people, you
should add a Content Warning.

Note: Psychological triggers are anything sensual or contextual that can cause flashbacks of traumatic events.

Everyone’s triggers are different and they can be highly specific, but Trigger Warnings don’t need to be.

What’s the Difference Between TWs and CWs?
- Trigger Warnings always outrank Content Warnings. If it is already covered with a TW,
you don’t need an extra CW.
- Think of It This Way: Being upset is manageable, while being thrown into a flashback
might cause serious harm to the person.

How To Use TWs and CWs
Purpose: To clearly define use of TWs and CWs.

When Should I Use a TW/CW?
- You should post TWs and CWs at the beginning or start of the content that requires them.
This allows the reader or viewer to make an educated decision if they want to engage
with the content.
- Make sure it’s clear what the warnings apply to — an introduction text usually doesn’t
need one, neither does pure mention of a trigger.
  - Example: You can use the words “death” or “animal abuse”, but any descriptions
of such should be put after a TW/CW.
- Potentially triggering content (after TWs) should be put behind a spoiler tag to black out
the text, video, or image.
- Potentially upsetting content (after CWs) only needs to be behind a spoiler if it feels
particularly heavy, intense, or drastic.

Using Spoiler Tags on Discord
- Use || at the beginning and end of the text you want to spoiler (e.g., ||Your text here||). The
text will appear blacked out and is only visible after you click on it.
- Do NOT spoiler the Trigger and Content Warnings themselves. That defies their
whole purpose!
- For images, use the following guides:
  - Mobile: How to Spoiler an Image on Discord Mobile: 2 Simple Methods
  - Desktop: How to Upload Images on Discord on PC or Mac: Easy Guide
- Examples of spoiler tags in action:

Space Today at 10:45 AM
Hey, y’all! Please give me feedback on my work.

TWs: Death, medical trauma
||Potentially triggering content here||

Space Today at 10:45 AM
Hey, y’all! Please give me feedback on my work.
CW and TWs: death, gore
||Potentially triggering and upsetting content here||

Lists of Common Trigger and Content Warnings
Purpose: To provide a list for reference of common TWs/CWs.
- Please refer to these lists for common Trigger and Content Warnings.
- Rule of Thumb: If you aren’t sure if it needs a TW or CW, err on the side of caution
rather than recklessness.

Common Trigger Warnings
- Hate, discrimination
- Any form of structural violence and oppression
- Sexual content, sexual abuse (“SA”), nudity, incest
- Any form of abuse (always note if it targets minors or children!)
- Common phobias (e.g. spiders, snakes, holes)
- Mental health problems (always note if it depicts eating disorders)
- Suicide (“SUI”), suicidal ideation (“SI”), self-harm (“SH”)
- Alcohol and drugs
- Miscarriage, stillbirth
- Blood, injuries, medical trauma
- Violent death, loss of loved ones, grief
- Torture, violence, crime (always note if if it depicts being the victim of it)
- Animal death and animal cruelty
- War, genocide
- Use of slurs

Common Content Warnings
- Pregnancy, childbirth
- Natural death
- Natural disasters
- Gore
- Minor violence, crime without victims, fictional crimes

- Non-explicit sexual jokes and references, not-nude erotic content
- Swearing or curse words

Why Should We Use TWs and CWs?
Purpose: To clearly define the importance of using TW/CWs.
- The importance of these warnings has always been the same. It isn’t a “new” invention.
- Consider This: People have engaged with upsetting or even triggering content before
people started using proper TWs and CWs, although they had no chance of knowing how
much it might harm them. Wouldn’t you rather make your content more accessible for
everyone and make sure people are in the right mindset to enjoy it?
- Everyone has things they’re sensitive about. Be considerate! It creates awareness and
takes a load off of everyone’s mind.
- Adding a TW, CW, and (if needed) a spoiler to your work costs you maybe 30 seconds.
It’s worth it. Encourage your friends and family to do the same!
- If you’re ever unsure about how to use TWs or CWs, please feel free to reach out to Page
One’s friendly moderator team, who’ll be able to assist (note: all questions may be
deferred back to Space for their expert opinion).
